古舟 · Ancient Boat

黑白

Two players and the sea

Face to face, Black and White. Between them, a sea that belongs to no one.

Player

Black

181 stones. Places the first stone of the game.

Player

White

180 stones. Plays after Black.

 

The sea

180 blue pieces. It does not play and belongs to no one: whoever captures changes it.

The board is not fixed

There are 361 shapes of the sea to start from. All of them leave 181 points in play.

Capturing transforms

Every captured stone forces a change to the sea: move a blue piece to any empty point, or remove it. But the sea never captures: no blue piece may leave a group with fewer than two liberties.

No agreements

At the end, what is on the board is counted. If you think one of your rival's stones cannot be saved, capture it before passing.

180 blue pieces + 181 points in play = 361, the full 19 × 19 board.

規則

The rules, with examples

If you know how to play Go, what is new is in steps 1, 2, 4, 5 and 7, because of the sea, and in step 8, where everything is decided on the board.

  1. 01

    Equipment

    A 19 × 19 Go board, 181 black stones, 180 white stones and 180 blue pieces. Captured stones go back to their owner's hand.

  2. 02

    Setup

    Choose one of the 361 shapes of the sea and place the 180 blue pieces in that shape. Points with a blue piece are not in play; the remaining 181 are.

  3. 03

    Turn

    Black starts. On your turn you place a stone on an empty point in play, or you pass. If you run out of stones, you can only pass.

  4. 04

    Liberties and capture

    A group is stones of the same color that are neighbors horizontally or vertically, not diagonally. Its liberties are the empty points in play that touch it. Blue pieces and the edge are not liberties. A group with no liberties leaves the board.

  5. 05

    The blue pieces

    The blue pieces are the sea: they belong to neither player, but whoever captures can change it. For every stone you capture you choose one of two: move any blue piece to any empty point (its old place comes into play) or remove a blue piece (its place comes into play and the piece does not come back). If there are no blue pieces left, there is nothing to decide. The limit: a blue piece cannot go to a point where some neighboring group, yours or your rival's, would be left with fewer than two liberties. That is why the sea squeezes but never captures, and there are no chain captures.

  6. 06

    No liberties

    You cannot place a stone that leaves your own group without liberties, unless that move captures your rival first. The sea does not need this rule: a blue piece never leaves a group with fewer than two liberties.

  7. 07

    Ko

    You cannot place a stone if the stones would be exactly as they were earlier in the game. Blue pieces do not count.

  8. 08

    End

    The game ends when both players pass in a row. You count one point for each of your stones and one for each point of your areas. An area is a set of empty points connected to each other; it is yours if all the stones that touch it are yours, even if it is also closed by blue pieces or the edge. If it touches stones of both colors, or no stones at all, it belongs to no one. White adds 7.5 points of compensation for playing second: there are no draws.

Read the complete rules
  1. Equipment. A 19 × 19 Go board, the stones of a Go set (181 black and 180 white) and 180 blue pieces. Captured stones go back to their owner's hand.
  2. Setup. The players agree on one of the 361 shapes of the sea and place the 180 blue pieces in that shape. Every shape leaves 181 points in play, is symmetrical, in one piece and has the center open. Points with a blue piece are not in play; the others (181) are.
  3. Turn. Black places the first stone, as in Go. On your turn you place a stone on an empty point in play, or you pass. If you run out of stones, you can only pass.
  4. Liberties and capture. Two points touch if they are neighbors horizontally or vertically, not diagonally. A group is the stones of the same color that touch each other. The liberties of a group are the empty points in play that touch it; blue pieces and the edge of the board are not liberties. A group with no liberties leaves the board.
  5. The blue pieces. For every stone you capture, you choose one of two: move any blue piece to any empty point, and its old place becomes a point in play; or remove a blue piece from the board, and its place becomes a point in play (the removed piece does not come back). You cannot leave the sea as it was; if there are no blue pieces left, there is no decision. A blue piece cannot go to a point where some neighboring group, of either color, would be left with fewer than two liberties. Decisions are made one at a time, and each one starts from the board as the previous one left it: the same piece may move more than once, and a piece may go into the gap of a stone just captured if it respects the limit. That is why the sea never captures and there are no chain captures.
  6. No liberties. You cannot place a stone that leaves your group without liberties, unless that move captures your rival first.
  7. Ko. You cannot place a stone if the stones would be exactly as they were earlier in the game. Blue pieces do not count.
  8. End. The game ends when both players pass in a row. What is on the board is counted, without removing anything by agreement: one point for each point with your stone and one for each point of your areas. An area is a set of empty points connected to each other horizontally or vertically. It is yours if all the stones that touch it are yours, even if it is also closed by blue pieces or the edge; if it touches stones of both colors, or no stones at all, it belongs to no one. Blue pieces count for no one. White adds 7.5 points of compensation (komi) for playing second, so there are no draws. If you think one of your rival's stones cannot be saved, capture it before passing. On the board it is enough to count one player's area and compare it with half of the counted points (the points in play, minus those of areas with no owner); see Counting the Chinese way.
開局

The 361 shapes of the sea

361 ways to start a game. All of them leave 181 points in play and 180 blue pieces, are symmetrical, in one piece and have the center open. No two are alike, not even rotated or reflected.

There is one shape for each point of the board, from A1 to T19. The name comes from the coordinate: the column gives the noun and the row the adjective. Tap any shape to see it large and play with it.

滄海column K = 海 searow 10 = 滄 deep blueK10 = Deep blue sea = Sokai

Each square is a shape and sits at its own coordinate. The columns go from the plainest lands (A) to the most worked ones (T); within each column, the simplest are at the bottom. In the center, K10, is 滄海. The circle, the octagon and the four gates are in row 1: they are A1, E1 and R1.

How they are named: the nineteen rows and columns

The readings come in this order: Chinese (pinyin), Japanese (on reading) and Korean. Row 1 is the bottom one.

    數子

    Counting the Chinese way

    How a finished game is counted on a real board, step by step.

    On a real board

    Sokai counts by area, like Chinese Go: stones and areas are worth the same. That is why there is no need to count both colors. It is enough to count the area of one and compare it with half of the counted points.

    1. Fill the neutral points. Before passing, it is worth placing stones on the empty points left between stones of both colors: each one is worth a point to whoever takes it.
    2. Count the counted points. The points in play are 181 plus the removed pieces; moving a piece does not change the total. If an area has no owner, because it touches stones of both colors or none, subtract all its points. What remains are the counted points.
    3. Work out half of the counted points.
    4. Count Black's area. There is no need to count the stones on the board: they are 181 minus those left in the bowl, because captured stones go back to it. Add the empty points of its areas, grouped in tens, as they do in China.
    5. Compare. If Black goes over half, its lead on the board is twice what it goes over by.
    6. Subtract the compensation. White adds 7.5 points for playing second. If Black's lead is 8 or more, Black wins; if it is less, White wins. There are no draws.

    Without counting White's area: if Black has 96 of 187, White has the remaining 91.

    天元

    Why 361

    The story of a number, of a point and of an astronomer who opened in the center.

    Legend has it that Emperor Yao invented Go to teach his son Danzhu how to think. It is only a legend, but it says something true: from very early on, in China the game was seen as a school for the mind. What we do know is that the board was not born at the size it has today.

    The oldest boards that survive, from the Han period, have 17 lines per side: 289 points. The game was played that way for centuries. The 19-line board, with its 361 points, took hold later, and by the Tang period it was the usual one. From that time come the boards the Japanese court kept in the Shōsōin treasury, in Nara, which still exist.

    Two more lines per side seem a small thing, but they changed the game: there is more room between the corners and the center, and the game stops being only a fight and also becomes a sharing out. And they brought a number that Chinese scholars found impossible to ignore.

    Three hundred sixty-one is three hundred sixty plus one. The Classic of Weiqi in Thirteen Chapters, written around 1050, in the Song period, reads it this way: the one is the origin, the central point, from which the four directions are governed; the remaining three hundred sixty stand for “the number of the turning of the heavens” (周天之數). The four corners are the four seasons, with ninety points each, like their days; the seventy-two points of the edge are the seventy-two five-day periods into which the year was divided; and the stones, half black and half white, are yin and yang. To play a game was, for them, to travel through a whole year.

    The Chinese called that central point 天元, tianyuan, “the origin of heaven”. The name traveled to Japan with the game, where it is pronounced tengen. The man who made it famous was Shibukawa Shunkai, a man with two trades. He was born into the Yasui house, one of the four that taught Go under the shogunate, and he was also an astronomer: he reformed the Japanese calendar after centuries of using the Chinese one.

    Shunkai looked at the board and saw the sky. If the center was the pole star, around which everything turns, the first stone had to go there. He put it to the test in one of the castle games, before the shōgun, against Hon'inbō Dōsaku, the strongest player of his time. He opened in the center and the game went to Dōsaku. It is said that he never tried it again. But the name remained, and the other eight marked points of the board are still called hoshi (星), “stars”.

    Sokai inherits that whole story. It keeps the 19-line board and its number: there are 361 shapes, one for each point. And it keeps the scholars' reading: in every shape the center is land, so the origin can always be played, and of the remaining three hundred sixty points, exactly half are sea. The shape at the center, K10, bears the name of the game: 滄海.

    九品

    The grades

    From 27k to 1k, in nine stages: from gazing at the sea to contemplating the sea and the fields.

    Why the count goes backwards. It is the Go count. Whoever starts has many kyu and leaves them behind: the smaller the number, the stronger the player. Sokai uses the same scale, from 27k to 1k.

    Why nine stages. Already in ancient China players were ranked in nine categories, the “nine grades of weiqi” (圍棋九品), each with its own name. Sokai divides its 27 kyu into nine stages of three.

    Where the names come from. Each stage names what the player learns to do with the sea, and together they tell a path. You begin by gazing at the sea from the shore, you learn to cross it, to give it shape and to measure its strength, and you end by contemplating “the sea and the fields” (滄桑), the image of the old saying that gives the game its name: everything changes.

    How you climb. Each stage is 100 points, about 33 per kyu. From 27k to 6k you climb by solving exercises; from 6k to 1k, by playing games.

    1. 3k–1k觀滄桑Contemplating the sea and the fieldsSees every future coast before it exists.
    2. 6k–4k移海Moving the seaWith each blue piece, squeezes the rival where it hurts most.
    3. 9k–7k開天Opening the skyKnows the shapes of the sea and chooses the one that suits.
    4. 12k–10k知潮Knowing the tideKnows when to move the sea and when to remove it.
    5. 15k–13k知止Knowing the limitKnows how far the sea can squeeze before placing the stone.
    6. 18k–16k守氣Keeping the breathKnows that the sea cannot squeeze a group with two liberties: only stones can.
    7. 21k–19k築岸Building the shoreUses the sea as shape, not as a wall.
    8. 24k–22k涉水Crossing the waterStarts using both options of the sea: moving it and removing it.
    9. 27k–25k望海Gazing at the seaKnows the rules and plays as in Go.

    What Sokai inherits from Go and what changes.

    Sokai is born from Go: the same board of points, the same stones, the same way of counting. They share almost all the rules; what changes is the sea.

    緣起

    The letter

    To my twin brother.

    Very soon we will complete fifteen thousand turns of the Earth on its axis. It is not a date that calendars mark, but it seemed to me worthy of a letter. I wanted to take the chance to tell you about something I have been working on in my spare time: a new game, made largely so that we can play it together.

    It all began a few years ago, when I watched AlphaGo, the documentary about the DeepMind program that beat Lee Sedol 4 to 1, one of the best Go players of our time. I only knew the very basics of the game, but its complexity caught me: it has more possible positions than there are atoms in the observable universe. For decades people believed that put it beyond the reach of a machine, and that frontier fell in five games.

    I wanted to understand that game. I started playing online, solved problems (tsumego) every morning and climbed in level until I reached 1 kyu (I think that is roughly the level at which you play chess, around 1950 to 2000 Elo). I bought several books by Kano Yoshinori, Kageyama Toshiro and Cho Chikun, three masters known for teaching the game step by step.

    As you know, the game led me to its culture: to the history of Buddhism, from India to the Chan (禪) of China and the Zen of Japan, and to chronicles such as the Blue Cliff Record (碧巖錄), which would end up giving names and stories to the shapes of this game. In time I went from reading about Zen to practicing it (zazen). A game of Go is played seated, in silence and attentive to every moment; that is why it is called shudan (手談), “conversation of hands”.

    There is one feature of Go that caught my attention: it always starts the same way. Every game begins on an empty, identical board, and over the centuries that has allowed an enormous theory of openings and corner sequences to build up. A Go player comes to each game with all of that memorized, just as in chess some people specialize in particular gambits and defenses. That led me to ask myself:

    Could one create a game with the spirit of Go, but with a board that changes and no openings to memorize?

    I have always had the habit of writing down ideas that seem promising, and I wrote that one down right away. Then other projects came along and the note was forgotten. Not long ago I found it while going through my notes, and this time I thought of us: of a game to share between brothers and to keep thinking together around a board.

    The result is called 滄海, Sokai, “the vast sea”. It is played on an ordinary Go board, but half of it is covered by sea: 180 blue pieces and 181 points of land. Each time someone captures a stone, they move a blue piece somewhere else or remove it, so the board changes over the course of the game. And there are 361 different ways to start, one for each point of the board, each with its own name and its own little story.

    To discover the game, I trained on my computer a companion I called 雲水 Unsui, “the apprentice”: that is the name given in Sōtō monasteries to monks in training. It began knowing only what Go is; it knew nothing about the sea, and it has learned it by playing against itself. It has already played more than fifteen thousand games, more than the days we have lived. And it keeps learning, just like us.

    What I would really like is for us to learn together. The site has lessons to start from scratch and thousands of exercises, and then you and I can play, calmly, a different shape each time.

    Happy fifteen thousand days.
